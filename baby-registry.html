Baby Registry Essentials: Must-Haves vs Nice-to-Haves

Open any registry checklist online and you will meet a list of two hundred, maybe three hundred, items — and a quiet voice that says: good parents buy all of this. Here is the truth from the other side of the newborn months: babies need far, far less than that list wants you to buy. In this guide, we sort the registry into three honest piles: the true must-haves, the nice-to-haves that genuinely earn their keep for some families, and the skip-or-wait list — plus the second-hand safety rules every parent should know before borrowing a single thing.

The golden rule: what a baby actually needs

Start with the whole philosophy in one breath. A newborn needs: a safe place to sleep, a way to be fed, diapers, clothes to be warm in, a car seat to travel in — and you. That is the complete list of true needs. Everything else ever sold to parents is comfort or convenience, and there's nothing wrong with comfort and convenience — as long as you choose them on purpose, for your life, instead of out of fear.

And "your life" is the key phrase. A third-floor walk-up apartment uses a registry differently than a house with a laundry room. A family with a long car commute needs different gear than a family that walks everywhere. So as we go through the categories, keep asking one question: does this solve a problem I actually have? One more practical trick before we start: put the big-ticket items on the registry even if you plan to buy them yourselves — most registries offer a completion discount near your due date, and it applies to the stroller and car seat too. Registries are tools. Make yours work for you.

Sleep: the must-haves

You need exactly one safe sleep space to start: a crib, a bassinet, or a play yard with a bassinet insert — what matters is a firm, flat mattress that fits snugly with no gaps. Add two or three fitted sheets, because laundry happens at 3 a.m., and three or four swaddles or wearable sleep sacks, which do the cozy job blankets can't safely do in a crib.

While we're here, the safe-sleep basics that shape the shopping: babies sleep on their backs, on that firm flat surface, with the sleep space kept bare — no pillows, no loose blankets, no bumpers, no stuffed animals in the crib. Many parents keep the sleep space in their own room for the early months. Notice what this means for your registry: the expensive crib bedding sets, with their quilts and bumpers, are decoration your baby won't use. Skip the set; buy the sheets. Sleep: sorted, simply.

Feeding: the must-haves

However feeding goes in your house, these earn their place. First, the unsung heroes: burp cloths — eight to ten of them, plain muslin is perfect. They will live on your shoulder, your couch, and every surface you own. Then bottles: start with a small set, maybe four to six bottles in one or two styles, because babies develop opinions and the brand you stockpiled may not be the brand your baby accepts. More can arrive in two days; a cupboard of rejected bottles cannot un-arrive.

If you plan to breastfeed: comfortable nursing bras or tanks, nursing pads, and a pump — many health insurance plans cover one, so that's worth a phone call before it's worth a purchase. If you plan to formula-feed: your bottles, a bottle brush, one container of formula to start — again, no stockpiling, since the type that suits your baby can change — and a simple way to make feeds at night without fully waking your brain. A nursing or feeding pillow belongs in the nice-to-have pile we'll meet soon; plenty of families do beautifully with a regular pillow. And a couple of pacifiers, if you'd like to offer them — small, cheap, and very much optional.

Diapering: the must-haves

Diapering is gloriously simple. Newborn-size diapers: one or two packs only. Some babies are born nearly too big for them, and all babies outgrow them at Formula One speed — after that, size ones are where a small stock-up makes sense, and even then, gift cards beat guesses. Wipes: a few packs of a gentle, unscented kind. A changing surface: a contoured pad on top of a dresser, or a simple foldable mat you can use anywhere — a dedicated changing table is pure nice-to-have. Add a basic diaper cream, a small lidded bin or diaper pail for the fragrant evidence, and a handful of the hand towels you're not emotionally attached to. That's the whole department. Everything beyond this is garnish.

Going out: the must-haves

To leave the house, your baby needs precisely one thing that is genuinely non-negotiable: a car seat. Choose an infant carrier seat or a convertible seat rated from newborn — buy it early, and install it well before your due date; our Hospital Bag guide walks through why the hospital won't send baby home without one. One base per car if you go the carrier route. Next, a way to move through the world: a stroller, or — our gentle suggestion — start with a simple wrap or soft carrier, which many newborns prefer anyway, and let your real walking life tell you which stroller you need later.

Your diaper bag deserves one calming sentence: any roomy bag with pockets works, and plenty of parents use a backpack they already own. Pack it with diapers, wipes, a change of clothes for the baby — and one for you, learned by every parent exactly once — and a couple of light receiving blankets, which serve as shade, spill control, and privacy in rotation. One safety note for the car seat: bulky coats and thick blankets don't belong under the harness straps — dress baby in thin layers and tuck the blanket over the buckled straps instead.

Health & everyday care: the must-haves

The care shelf, short and sensible. One true essential: a digital thermometer — when a tiny baby feels warm at midnight, "seems hot" is not information, and a thermometer is. Add a nasal aspirator and saline drops for the stuffy-nose nights, baby nail scissors or a soft emery board — newborn nails are tiny, sharp, and surprisingly fast-growing — and a gentle, fragrance-free baby wash with soft washcloths. A simple baby bathtub or sink insert makes bath time easier and safer-feeling, especially while you're learning.

And now a liberation: you do not need a baby pharmacy. Infant medicines are dosed by weight and age, and what to have on hand is a question for your pediatrician at the first visits — ask them, and buy exactly what they suggest, fresh, when it's actually needed. The same spirit covers laundry: most families simply wash baby clothes in their regular routine; there's no magic required. Health and care: a shelf, not a store.

Nice-to-haves: lovely, not required

Pile two: the nice-to-haves — genuinely useful, entirely optional. A bouncer or swing: a safe place to set the baby down while you shower or cook, and some babies adore them. A white noise machine, which recreates the womb's whoosh and can rescue naps. A baby monitor — audio-only is plenty for many homes; video if your layout makes you anxious without it. A play mat for tummy time, a second baby carrier so both parents have one adjusted to them, a feeding pillow, a nightlight that's warm and dim for night feeds.

Read this pile with your own life in mind. Night owl in a small apartment? The white noise machine might be your family's best friend. Two-story house? The monitor jumps the queue. Nice-to-have means one thing: lovely if it's gifted, completely painless if it's skipped. Nothing in this pile stands between your baby and thriving — it stands between you and a slightly easier Tuesday.

Skip or wait: the honest pile

Pile three — skip it, or wait and see. Newborn shoes: heart-melting, function-free; tiny feet need socks and footed sleepers, not footwear. Mountains of newborn-size clothing: some babies skip the size entirely, and every baby outgrows it in weeks — a week's worth of simple sleepers and onesies is the sweet spot, because laundry exists. Wipe warmers, fancy diaper disposal systems with proprietary refills, shopping-cart covers, elaborate bottle gadgets — the pattern to notice is that these solve problems you may never have.

Wait, also, on the big toy haul: for the first months, your baby's favorite entertainment is your face, your voice, and high-contrast patterns. And wait on bulk-buying anything consumable in one size or one formula brand — babies change sizes and preferences at high speed. The golden rule of this pile: in the age of two-day delivery, you can buy the solution when the problem actually shows up. A shorter registry today is not a risk. It's a strategy.

Second-hand & borrowing: the safety rules

Now, the smart parent's superpower: second-hand. Babies use things for weeks, so borrowing and buying used is brilliant — with a few non-negotiable rules. Great second-hand: clothes, always — wash and they're new again. Swings and bouncers, if the straps and frame are sound. Strollers, after checking brakes and the fold. Books, play mats, most toys that can be washed or wiped.

Never second-hand, full stop: car seats. A used seat comes with an invisible history — it may have been in a crash that weakened it without leaving a mark, it may be expired, since car seats have expiration dates, and its manual and parts are often missing. This is the one purchase where new is the safety rule. Cribs with an unknown history belong on the same caution list: safety standards have changed over the years, older cribs may not meet current rules, and missing hardware is a real hazard — if a crib comes from a recent, trusted source with all its parts and instructions, many families pair it with a new mattress; when in doubt, choose new. And for any used gear, take two minutes to check the model against the official recall lists before it enters your home. Borrowed, safe, and checked: that's the formula.

✅ The registry, honestly sorted — at a glance

  • Sleep: one safe sleep space, 2–3 fitted sheets, 3–4 swaddles or sleep sacks
  • Feeding: 8–10 burp cloths, 4–6 bottles to start, nursing basics or formula basics
  • Diapering: 1–2 packs of newborn diapers, gentle wipes, a changing pad or mat, basic cream
  • Going out: a NEW car seat, a wrap or carrier, any roomy bag
  • Care: a digital thermometer, aspirator + saline, nail scissors, gentle wash
  • Skip or wait: newborn shoes, clothing mountains, wipe warmers, bulk stockpiles

Key takeaways

  • True needs fit in one breath: safe sleep, food, diapers, warmth, a car seat — and you
  • Buy small and let real life decide: babies reject brands and outgrow sizes at high speed
  • Second-hand is smart for clothes, swings and strollers — but a car seat is always bought new
  • In the age of two-day delivery, a shorter registry isn't a risk — it's a strategy
