Pregnancy Week 33: Your Baby's Bones Are Hardening

Welcome to week 33, mama — the week your baby's skeleton gets serious. Those soft bones are hardening day by day, while one beautiful exception stays flexible by design. And there's a quieter miracle happening too: your immune system is handing your baby a starter kit of protection, antibody by antibody. This week we'll cover your baby's growth, the great gift of immunity, your body's new waddle-and-balance physics, why water is suddenly your best friend, and the warning signs to keep close. Here's your complete guide to week 33.

Your baby this week

Your baby now weighs a little over four pounds — closing in on two kilos — and measures about seventeen inches long: about the size of a pineapple. Crown, leaves, and all — though your version is considerably cuddlier and much more opinionated about your sleeping position. The bones are the week's headline.

Throughout your baby's body, the skeleton is hardening — cartilage steadily becoming bone — which is part of why calcium matters in these weeks. But one region plays by different rules, and brilliantly: the bones of the skull are not fusing together. They remain separate, flexible plates with soft gaps between them, so the head can mold and shift as it travels the birth canal, and so the brain still has room to grow.

Hardening everywhere it should, flexible exactly where it must be — nature's engineering at its most elegant. And then there's the gift. Your baby's immune system is developing, and it isn't building it alone: antibodies from your body — especially the IgG antibodies that carry your immune memory of infections and vaccinations — are crossing the placenta to your baby in increasing amounts during these final weeks.

It's a borrowed shield: protection your baby will carry into their first months of life, while their own immune system learns the ropes. It's one of the deep reasons the full stretch of the third trimester is so valuable — every week, more protection crosses over. Other week-33 developments: the amount of amniotic fluid around your baby peaks around now, so swimming space is technically at its maximum even as it feels full — your baby is simply bigger in the pool.

The pupils respond to light; the senses stay busy; the breathing practice continues; and fat keeps layering on at the steady half-pound-a-week pace, smoothing skin and rounding cheeks. Your baby also spends more time in organized sleep cycles now — quiet sleep, active sleep, dream sleep — waking and dozing on a schedule that is entirely theirs and rarely aligned with yours. And your baby's senses are increasingly social: familiar voices — yours above all — remain the favorite programming, and many babies at this stage respond to a hand resting on the belly, a particular song, or the evening quieting of the house.

Much of the weight arriving now is the good, insulating kind, including the stores that will power your baby's first days of adjusting to a world that is colder, brighter, and hungrier than the one they're leaving. This is a baby packing for the trip, too.

Your body & symptoms: the physics of the waddle

Your side, week 33. The waddle has arrived in its final form, and let's defend it: your pelvis is widening and loosening in preparation, your center of gravity has moved house entirely, and walking with a wider, rolling gait is not a failure of grace — it's structural engineering. Balance is genuinely different now, so stairs get a handrail, showers get care, and heels get an honorable retirement for the season.

Also on the roster: Braxton Hicks contractions, possibly more frequent; heartburn and breathlessness, sharing the crowded-top-floor problem; back, hip, and pelvic aches; leg cramps and restless legs at night; mild swelling; itchy stretched skin; colostrum for some; and sleep in its fragmented, pillow-fort edition. You may also notice you're warmer than everyone around you — a faster metabolism and increased blood flow will do that — and that your brain feels gloriously focused on some things and gently foggy on others. Lists are your friend.

Everyone's lists wander in week 33. A few more members of this week's cast deserve naming: tingling or achy hands — that fluid-in-tight-spaces effect again — which often eases with wrist support and a chat with your doctor; hemorrhoids and varicose veins for some moms, where gentle movement, not standing or sitting in one position for hours, and fiber with fluids are the household comforts; and a ribcage that may feel permanently occupied, because it is. You are, at this point, a home that someone is actively renovating from the inside, on their own schedule.

The noise and the dust settle eventually. The resident is worth it. And the movement report: kicks, rolls, and stretches should still arrive in your baby's familiar rhythm — strong, regular, unmistakably them. If that rhythm ever clearly changes, you know the drill, and we'll say it out loud again in the warning signs: same-day call, every time.

Water: the unglamorous hero

A love letter, briefly, to hydration — because in week 33, water quietly works a double shift. Your body is maintaining that peak level of amniotic fluid, running a blood volume roughly half again its pre-pregnancy size, and trying to keep digestion, swelling, and temperature all regulated at once. Steady fluid intake through the day supports every one of those projects.

The simplest approach most moms use: keep water within arm's reach, drink regularly rather than in rare floods, and let pale-yellow urine be your rough household gauge that you're on track. How much, exactly? Guidance varies by source, climate, and person — many references land around eight to twelve cups of total fluid a day — so treat that as a general landscape, and ask your doctor what's right for you, especially if you've been given any specific fluid guidance.

A practical footnote for the sleep-deprived: some moms front-load their drinking earlier in the day and ease off in the last hour or two before bed, buying themselves slightly longer stretches between bathroom trips. The baby will still schedule some. But fewer is fewer.

And a small myth to retire while we're here: mild swelling is not a reason to drink less — in fact, steady hydration generally helps your body manage fluid better. Sudden or severe swelling is a different matter entirely, and it's on our warning list in a moment, where it belongs. Remember, too, that water arrives in disguise: soups, fruits, yogurt, and watery vegetables all contribute to the day's total, and a squeeze of citrus or a few berries in the bottle can rescue plain water from boredom. Sip steadily, keep the bottle visible — hydration, like most of this trimester's wisdom, works best as a gentle background habit rather than an occasional grand gesture.

Getting ready: the final preparations begin

Week 33 is also when preparation turns from lists to logistics. The hospital bag, if it isn't packed, gets its finishing touches now — our Hospital Bag post in this series has the full item-by-item checklist. The car seat graduates from box to installed-and-checked, ideally with a practice run at buckling an empty seat without a crying audience.

The bassinet or crib is assembled, the first tiny clothes are washed, and the freezer may begin accumulating easy meals for the fourth trimester. It's also a fine week for the people-plan: who will be told when labor starts, who might care for older children or pets, and who's on the meals-and-visitors roster afterward. And keep the simple document folder going — insurance details, birth preferences, your doctor's after-hours number — because future you, in labor, appreciates past you's filing system enormously.

None of this needs to be perfect, and none of it needs to be finished by Friday. Think of it as lowering the activation energy of your future calm. One drawer, one phone call, one washed onesie at a time.

Two more decisions ripen nicely this week. If you haven't chosen where your baby will be seen after birth — a pediatrician or family practice — now is a comfortable time to ask friends, your doctor, and your insurance list for names; many practices welcome a short prenatal call or visit. And this is a kind stretch to talk through birth preferences with your doctor, while there's still leisure to ask follow-ups — our Birth Plan post in this series walks through the questions, and bringing a draft to a checkup turns it into a genuinely useful conversation rather than a document filed and forgotten.

⚠️ Warning signs — call your doctor the same day

Preterm labor, for any week before 37 weeks: regular contractions that keep coming, strengthen, or close in on each other · pelvic pressure like the baby pushing down · a dull low backache that won’t ease · menstrual-like cramps · a change in vaginal discharge — watery, mucus-like, or bloody · any vaginal bleeding · leaking fluid that might be your water breaking — a call right away. Movement: a clear decrease in your baby’s movements, or a pattern that feels different from normal — always a same-day call; this is the most important habit of the trimester, and it never expires. Preeclampsia, screened at every visit through your blood pressure and urine: a severe headache that doesn’t ease with rest · vision changes — spots, blurring, flashing lights · sudden swelling of your face or hands, or swelling that comes on fast (mild ankle swelling over a long day is common; sudden facial swelling is a call) · pain high in your belly, especially under your right ribs · sudden, rapid weight gain · a fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher · painful urination · a fall or blow to the belly. And call emergency services for chest pain or severe breathlessness, always. Most moms never need a single call from this list. But your team would rather get a hundred careful calls than one late one — and so would we.
