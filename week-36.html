Pregnancy Week 36: Your Baby May Drop Any Day

Welcome to week 36, mama — the last full month of pregnancy has begun, and one of its famous events could happen any day now: your baby may drop. It's called lightening, and it is exactly what it sounds like — your baby settling lower into your pelvis, head pointing at the exit, while your lungs suddenly remember what a full breath feels like. This week also brings a new rhythm of care for many women — weekly checkups — the group B strep screening if it hasn't happened yet, and the perfect moment for the final readiness checks: hospital bag, car seat, and the who's-doing-what list. Here's your complete guide to week 36.

Your baby this week

Your baby now weighs about six pounds and stretches roughly eighteen and a half inches long — about the size of a head of romaine lettuce. The main project remains the same as last week: growing. Fat is still layering on at an impressive pace, rounding out those arms and legs and building the insulation your baby will need in a world that — unlike their current one — does not come thermostat-controlled at a perfect, constant warmth.

Practice is the other headline. Your baby has been working on the sucking reflex for weeks, and by now many babies have been caught on ultrasound with a thumb in their mouth, rehearsing the exact skill they'll use at their very first meal. Swallowing is coordinated alongside it — a few weeks from now, your baby will combine sucking, swallowing, and breathing in the right order, which sounds simple and is actually a small masterpiece of coordination.

Breathing movements continue in their steady rhythm, exercising lungs that are now producing nearly all the surfactant they'll need — the substance that helps the tiny air sacs open and stay open with those first real breaths of air. And the brain, as ever, is outpacing everything: this week it is busy wrapping nerve fibers in their insulating layer — the process that lets signals travel fast and clean — a project so important that it continues well into your baby's childhood. Layer by layer, system by system, your baby is in the finishing room now — not quite finished, but unmistakably close.

And the bones throughout your baby's body are hardening nicely — all except the skull, whose plates stay soft and separate on purpose, designed to shift and flex for the journey through the birth canal. Nature left that one adjustable on purpose. Now for the position report.

Most babies are head-down by this point, and around now many begin to descend — dropping lower into the pelvis, settling into the neighborhood of the exit. For first-time mothers, this "dropping" often happens a few weeks before labor; for women who've given birth before, it may not happen until labor itself. Both are normal patterns.

If your baby is still bottom-down or sideways at this stage, don't panic and don't Google at 2 a.m. — this is a conversation for your doctor, who will talk you through what they recommend and what the options are. Babies also still have room for small adjustments, and your team checks position at every visit now. Meanwhile, those sleep-and-wake cycles keep their schedule — and those rolls, pushes, and stretches you feel during their waking hours should keep their familiar pattern. Snug quarters, same baby.

Your body & symptoms: the lightening trade

And you, mama — week 36 offers a famous trade, and most women who've been here will tell you exactly how it goes. When your baby drops, your lungs get their space back: breathing becomes noticeably easier, and heartburn often eases, because your stomach is no longer quite so compressed. It's one of the genuine reliefs of the home stretch, and it can arrive quite suddenly — one morning you simply take a deep breath and realize it worked.

The trade is what's happening downstairs. With your baby's head settled lower in your pelvis, pelvic pressure increases — a heavy, downward feeling, sometimes with sharp little twinges as the baby's head presses on nerves. Bathroom trips multiply, because your bladder now has even less room than it did in week 35, which is saying something.

The waddle becomes official: your center of gravity has moved, your joints are loose, and your walk has adapted, exactly as it should. Many women also notice an increase in vaginal discharge around now, which is usually a normal part of the body's preparation; anything that worries you about it is always worth a mention at your now-weekly visits. Also still on the roster: Braxton Hicks practice contractions, which may feel stronger and more frequent — still irregular, still easing with rest, hydration, and a change of position.

Sleep, which continues to be its own adventure of pillows, bathroom trips, and a mind rehearsing everything at 3 a.m. And the nesting instinct, which may hit its peak: if you find yourself organizing the pantry by category at thirty-six weeks, you are in excellent company. Ride the energy in short, gentle bursts — then sit down, feet up, and remember that the most important preparation is a rested mother. The baby is doing their final growing. Your job is fuel, rest, and showing up to those checkups.

Weekly checkups & the GBS screening

The headline of the calendar now: for many women, prenatal visits become weekly somewhere around week 36, and stay weekly until the birth. Every practice has its own rhythm, so ask yours what to expect — but one thing is certain: you and your care team are about to become very well acquainted. Each visit runs the familiar, reassuring route: blood pressure and urine checked, weight and belly growth measured, baby's heartbeat listened to, and a check of your baby's position.

Short appointments, long value — those quiet blood-pressure and urine checks are still screening for preeclampsia every single time, right up to delivery day. If your group B strep test hasn't happened yet, this is the week it most likely will — the screening window generally runs from about week 35 to week 37. Quick refresher, if you missed week 35's post: group B strep is a common bacteria many healthy adults carry without symptoms, the test is a gentle swab that takes a few seconds, and if you test positive, the only change is that IV antibiotics during labor join your birth plan — standard, calm, and handled entirely by your team.

Nothing about it is a verdict on you or your baby; it's simply one more way your team prepares. One practical tip for this weekly rhythm: keep a running note on your phone of every question that pops into your head between visits — the 3 a.m. ones count double — and bring the list with you. "Is this normal?" is a complete and respectable question at week 36, and no question about your body, your baby, or your birth is too small for the people whose entire job is you two.

These visits are also where the final birth conversations happen. If your birth preferences are written — and our Birth Plan post in this series can help you finish that one calm page — bring the final version now, and talk through anything still undecided with your doctor. Ask the practical questions too: whom do you call when labor starts, day or night?

Where exactly do you go? What does your doctor want you to do when contractions become regular? Every birthplace answers a little differently, and hearing your own team's answers, from them, is the kind of certainty that lets you sleep a little better in these final weeks.

The final checks: bag, car seat & home base

Which brings us to the most satisfying checklist of pregnancy: the final readiness sweep. First, the hospital bag — by week 36, the classic advice is to have it packed and parked by the door. If yours isn't yet, our Hospital Bag post in this series walks you through every item, for you and for baby.

Add the chargers, copies of your paperwork, and the going-home outfit in a newborn and a slightly bigger size — babies arrive in the size they choose, not the size we predict. Second, the car seat — the one item you truly cannot improvise. Many hospitals won't discharge a baby without one, and installing it for the first time in a hospital car park, sleep-deprived, is an experience nobody recommends.

So: install it this week, in the vehicle that will actually bring the baby home. Read the manufacturer's instructions carefully — every seat is a little different — and if your community offers free car-seat safety checks, as many fire stations and hospitals do, take them up on it. Third, the home base: a route to the hospital you both know, the phone list — doctor, birthplace, your support people — saved where your partner can find it at 3 a.m., a few easy meals in the freezer, and a plan for pets and older children, made with love and confirmed twice.

Check, check, check. You are officially the kind of ready that's left: the kind where the lists are done, and what remains is simply waiting to meet someone.

⚠️ Warning signs — call your doctor the same day

Preeclampsia, screened at every visit for a reason — call the same day, right away if symptoms feel strong, for: a severe headache that doesn’t ease with rest · vision changes such as spots, blurring, or flashing lights · sudden swelling of your face or hands · pain high in your belly, especially under your right ribs · sudden, rapid weight gain. Also call right away for: a clear decrease in your baby’s movements, or a movement pattern that feels different from their normal — day or night, and never explained away by a “dropped” baby, whose pattern should stay the same · your water breaking — a trickle or a gush you can’t stop — especially before 37 weeks, or if the fluid looks green or brown or has a strong smell · any vaginal bleeding · regular, strengthening contractions that keep coming before 37 weeks · a fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher. And call emergency services for severe breathlessness or chest pain, which are never wait-and-see symptoms, in pregnancy or out of it. Most moms never meet a single item on this list — but every mom should know it cold, because with these signs, calling early is what changes outcomes, and your team would far rather get the call.

  • A route to the hospital you both know, a few easy meals in the freezer, and a plan for pets and older children — made with love and confirmed twice
  • Keep a running note on your phone of every question between visits — the 3 a.m. ones count double — and bring the list

Key takeaways

  • Lightening is a trade: easier breathing and less heartburn, in exchange for more pelvic pressure and bathroom trips
  • Visits go weekly around now — and the GBS screening window (weeks 35–37) closes soon if yours hasn't happened
  • The final checks: bag by the door, car seat installed and checked, phone list where your partner can find it
  • A dropped baby's movement pattern should stay exactly the same — a change is always a call
